# Candidate lines

Working rule I set myself before drafting: the line has to hook on the first four or
five words, turn once, and pay off on the last word. No pun-only lines, no "my therapist
said" formats, no joke shapes a reader has already met on a thousand timelines.

## Drafts

1. My grandmother has outlived three husbands and one very confident rooster.
   *Idea: a woman so durable the animals lose their nerve. Turn sits on "very confident",
   which is the only adjective in the sentence doing work. Two items, not three, so the
   rooster arrives as a swerve instead of a list.*

2. A wasp has been watching me cook for a week, and yesterday she brought a smaller wasp,
   and I am beginning to suspect I am the apprentice.
   *Idea: the narrator is being trained by insects. Genuinely fresh and the reversal is
   physical, but it takes three clauses to arrive and the reader has to re-read who is
   teaching whom. Strong, slightly overloaded.*

3. The pigeon on my windowsill has visited every morning for three years and has never once
   asked what I do for a living, which is why I tell it everything.
   *Idea: the ideal friend is the one who never asks about your job. Warm, specific,
   real turn. But the hook is slow and the payoff is a feeling rather than a laugh.*

4. The last thing my grandfather planted was a row of onions he knew he would never eat,
   and every spring we cry over them anyway.
   *Idea: grief disguised as a gardening joke. Moving and tight. Honest worry: the whole
   thing rests on one pun, and a judge in a hurry may file it under "clever".*

5. I keep a list of things I will do when I am braver, and it is mostly ordering the fish.
   *Idea: a whole timid life in one restaurant order. Compact and charming. Truthfully it
   is a small observation, not a surprise; it smiles rather than lands.*

6. My roommate labels everything in the fridge - milk, eggs, butter, "my grief" - and the
   last one has been there since March.
   *Idea: deadpan escalation into the absurd. The label list is funny, the date is funnier.
   But the "named emotion in the fridge" move is a well-worn millennial-caption shape.*

7. The museum in my town has a painting labelled "Unknown Woman, Unknown Artist," and my
   mother has been telling everyone it is her for eleven years.
   *Idea: vanity as a lifelong project. Good character, good last clause. Slightly long to
   set up for the size of the laugh.*

8. There is a bakery in my town that sells yesterday's bread as "tomorrow's bread" and
   nobody has ever complained.
   *Idea: retail euphemism taken to its logical fraud. Nice and dry, but the joke is a
   labelling trick and stops there.*

9. I applied for a passport photo so many times that the clerk keeps my picture in her
   wallet, which is either very kind or a security issue.
   *Idea: bureaucratic intimacy. The last clause is a good hinge, but "either X or Y"
   endings are a format, and the setup is doing too much work.*

10. My father built a bookshelf one inch too short for every book we own, and he calls it
    "a lesson," and we have never once discussed what the lesson is.
    *Idea: dad logic as a family religion. The final clause is the best writing here, but
    "dad builds a bad thing and calls it character" is a familiar trope.*

11. I have been pretending to be a regular at a diner for two years, and yesterday the
    waitress said "the usual?" and I have not slept since.
    *Idea: a small lie growing teeth. Funny and clean. The premise is a known anxiety bit,
    and "I have not slept since" is doing the punch alone.*

12. My dog has a better credit score than I do, and I have decided not to ask how.
    *Idea: the pet has a secret financial life. Reliable laugh, good deadpan. But it is a
    one-beat mismatch, the kind of line that gets a "ha" and no re-read.*

13. I have decided that if I am ever on a sinking ship, I will be the one who says "it is
    fine" in a calm voice, and I have been practising in the bath.
    *Idea: rehearsing disaster composure in two inches of water. Likeable, self-aware,
    and the bath is the right image. The joke is about the narrator only, though; nothing
    unexpected happens to the reader.*

14. The cat and I have reached an agreement: I feed her, she sits on my keyboard, and
    neither of us mentions the bird.
    *Idea: a household conspiracy with a shared crime. Charming and tight. Cat jokes are
    the most crowded shelf in the shop, and this one lives there.*

15. In my hometown the library has a "returns" shelf for books and a "regrets" shelf for
    everything else, and my mother has a card for both.
    *Idea: an invented civic absurdity with a character to match. Fresh institution, good
    last beat. But it needs the reader to build the whole library in their head, and the
    laugh is architectural rather than immediate.*

16. The best four seconds of my day are when the microwave says "enjoy your meal" and
    means it more than anyone else in the building.
    *Idea: the appliance as the last honest well-wisher. Lovely and a little sad. Twee
    risk is real, and it is a sentiment, not a turn.*

## Kept three

- **1 (grandmother / rooster).** Hook in three words, one sentence, one swerve, punch on
  the final noun. Nothing to explain, nothing borrowed. It is funny because of what it
  refuses to say.
- **2 (wasp apprentice).** The freshest idea in the pile and the only one that makes the
  reader revise the scene. Held back only by its length and the half-beat of parsing.
- **3 (pigeon).** The warmest and most human; the twist is a small philosophy of
  friendship rather than a gag. Would be my pick for a different judge.

## Honest cuts

4 and 12 are the near-misses: 4 is beautiful but pun-dependent, 12 is a clean laugh with
no second life. 6, 10, 11, 14 are all well-built and all recognizable shapes - the kind of
line a judge has read before lunch. 5, 7, 8, 9, 13, 15, 16 are decent writing with soft
endings; 8 and 16 in particular are pleasant rather than entertaining.

## Chosen

1 - My grandmother has outlived three husbands and one very confident rooster.

It is the only line here that is over before the reader can get comfortable, and the
rooster is the only word they will still be thinking about ten seconds later.
